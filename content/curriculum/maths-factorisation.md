# Factorisation of Algebraic Expressions

Factorisation means writing an expression as a product of its factors. It is the reverse of expanding brackets.

## Common factor method

Take out the highest common factor of all terms.

Example: 6x^2 + 9x = 3x(2x + 3).

## Difference of two squares

The pattern a^2 - b^2 = (a - b)(a + b) is one of the most useful in algebra.

Example: x^2 - 9 = x^2 - 3^2 = (x - 3)(x + 3).

Because 9 is 3 squared, the expression is a difference of squares.

## Factorising quadratics

For x^2 + bx + c, find two numbers that multiply to c and add to b.

Example: x^2 + 5x + 6. The numbers 2 and 3 multiply to 6 and add to 5, so x^2 + 5x + 6 = (x + 2)(x + 3).

## Why factorisation matters

Once a quadratic is factorised, you can find its roots by setting each factor to zero. For x^2 - 9 = 0, the factors give x - 3 = 0 or x + 3 = 0, so x = 3 or x = -3. A hard problem becomes two easy ones.

## Common mistakes

- Forgetting to take out the full highest common factor.
- Mixing up the signs in the difference of squares.
- Not checking by expanding the brackets back out.
